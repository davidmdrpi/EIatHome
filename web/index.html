5. Track B: radio tokens

A k-means tokenizer turns each frame into one of K “radio tokens”. Or pick the pinned tokenizer, which fits nothing: a frame is token 0 if no channel reaches 6 noise sigma, and otherwise the 2-channel bin of its brightest channel. Then we ask whether the token stream behaves like a language, compared with surrogates: shuffled (each snippet’s tokens in random order) and Markov-1 (same pair statistics, nothing longer).

The frames are split into training, validation and test parts before anything is fitted: the tokenizer and both surrogates learn from the training part only, and every held-out score uses the test part. No statistic looks across the boundary of a snippet, slice or split. Real data uses one fixed window per slice and holds out whole scans, or whole sessions once there are three.

Each snippet or slice is compressed on its own, so short segments pay DEFLATE’s fixed overhead. It’s the same for every stream: compare the streams with each other, not with an absolute size.

Does it speak back? A token language model

A small causal model learns to predict the next radio token: a GRU (16-dimensional token embeddings, 32 hidden units) or a transformer (one block of single-head causal attention with learned positions for up to 32 tokens), sized to about the same number of parameters. It uses the same splits as the n-gram model above: it trains on the training split for up to 10 epochs, keeps the epoch that does best on the validation split, and is scored once on the test split. Then it continues the first test segment from its first half (at most 16 tokens). The transformer stops when its input would exceed the 32 positions it was trained on, so it never shifts a token onto an untrained position. Each token is drawn as its k-means centroid frame, or for pinned tokens as a line in its bin. With pinned tokens the transformer starts from the merge anchor’s fixed weights θ₀ instead of a random start, so its runs can be merged (see the Work units tab).

Merging token models

        
Many volunteers could each train a small model and combine the results, if their models start from the same weights and read the same tokens. This unit tests that on one machine. It trains Track B’s token transformer in three sets of 4 runs: 4 seeds on all the training text; 4 workers each on its own quarter of it, from one pinned start; and the same 4 quarters from a short shared warm start. Then it merges what each set learned and scores everything on text none of them saw.

        
Spec , anchor : the Rosetta text as FSK, in pinned tokens, split in text order into training, validation and test parts. Each worker trains for up to 20 epochs and keeps its best validation epoch; the warm start is the anchor’s θ₀ trained 3 epochs on all the training text. Each set’s weight changes are merged five ways: a plain average; DARE (drop 90% of each change at random and scale the rest up 10 times, which is how a change could be sent compactly) then the average; TIES (keep each change’s largest 20%, elect each weight’s sign, average only the changes that agree); DARE then TIES; and DARE then TIES dividing by all 4 workers instead of the ones that agree. The card carries the scores and a summary of the changes, not the weights, so it fits in an issue link. A run takes about a minute. (Cards of the earlier trackb-merge/v0, the seeds set alone, are still accepted.)

✓ marks the worker you’d keep without merging: the best on validation. From θ₀, a random start, a weight change isn’t a small adjustment that can be dropped: it’s what the model learned, so dropping 90% of each change (DARE) or keeping only the largest 20% (TIES) loses most of it. DARE then TIES can cost far more than an untrained model: DARE scales the changes it keeps up 10 times so that their sum stays right on average, but TIES averages only the changes that survive, so each one stays 10 times too large. Dividing by all the workers instead removes that excess.

Blinded signal recovery

        
Can a detector find a weak signal without raising more false alarms? A recovery unit hides 756 synthetic signals in real off-source data from a Voyager 1 observation, gives the windows to two standard detectors without saying which hold what, and only then checks which they found. Each detector’s threshold was fixed in advance on a different scan, so that it flags 1% (or 0.1%) of windows with nothing in them.

        
Spec . The windows: 1,024 cuts of 49 frames × 64 channels from an off-source scan (2020-07-16, Green Bank), with their real noise and interference. The signals: five classes (drifting tones, intermittent tones, wideband signals, a carrier with sidebands, frequency-shift keying) and two interference-like decoys (a zero-drift line, a one-frame broadband pulse), 18 of each at six strengths, plus 268 windows left empty. Strength is the SNR of a matched filter that knows the exact signal. Every unit number draws different signals into the same windows.

Real data: Voyager 1 and off-source micro-slices

        
Checking for published slices…

        
Instead of multi-gigabyte HDF5 files, the Live Lab uses micro-slices: 16 time steps × 256 channels around each signal, about 16 KB each. They’re cut from the Breakthrough Listen observation by tools/extract_slices.py and published in web/data/slices/.

        
The off-source slices come from the same on/off cadence with the telescope pointed 1° away from Voyager, cut where Voyager’s signals would have drifted to by then. They’re real receiver noise and interference with nothing in the beam: if a model scores as well on them as on Voyager, it isn’t learning the signal.

What is this?

        
EI@Home flips the usual SETI machine-learning pipeline. Instead of training a filter to throw away interference, it uses candidate signals as training data and asks what the models learn. This page runs the experiments directly in your browser, on your own hardware, with nothing to install.

        
Track A: continuous dynamics

        
A small model learns to predict the next spectrogram frame: a GRU, an LSTM or one layer of causal attention, each with about the same number of parameters. We compare it with simple baselines, look at its hidden states, and check whether its state encodes physical quantities such as drift rate.

        
Track B: is it language?

        
A tokenizer turns frames into discrete tokens, and we measure entropy, mutual information, compressibility and Zipf statistics against surrogates. The Rosetta control (real English text sent as FSK) checks that the pipeline can find language when it is there. A Zipf-like curve on its own proves nothing.

        
The Observatory viewport

        
The sky map above the Live Lab and Recovery tabs shows where each signal comes from: Voyager 1 as the Green Bank Telescope pointed at it in 2016 and 2020, the off-source positions 1° away, and a few well-known targets for reference. When you pick a signal or start a computation it slews there, and zooms toward the telescope’s beam as the work progresses. Synthetic signals are marked low over the Green Bank horizon, since they come from your browser, not the sky. Drag to look around, scroll to zoom, and click a marker to select its signal. Observatory mode fills the screen and can keep running work units.

        
Star positions, magnitudes and colours: the Hipparcos catalogue (ESA 1997). Constellation figures, names and IAU boundaries: d3-celestial by Olaf Frohn (BSD-3-Clause). Pointings: the Breakthrough Listen files’ headers.

        
Blinded recovery

        
Before a model’s output can mean anything about unknown signals, we need to know which signals it would have found. The Recovery tab hides synthetic signals of known class and strength in real off-source data, gives them to detectors without the answers, and counts what they found at a false-alarm rate fixed in advance. When you submit a unit it’s recomputed, and then it joins the combined recovery curves.

        
Honest limits

        
    
          
  • Voyager is a positive control, not a candidate: we know exactly what it is. The off-source scans are its negative control.
    • 
          
  • Synthetic signals are simple. Real signals differ, and that difference is worth investigating before concluding anything.
    • 
          
  • Different compute backends give slightly different numbers. Replication compares results within a tolerance.
    •
